José Vidal

Where engineering meets systems thinking.

I build bespoke solutions to difficult problems, moving between mechanical design, embedded systems, infrastructure and AI as the problem demands. My work is driven by understanding how things actually behave — then finding ways to make them work.

My background runs from mechanical design engineering through embedded systems, enterprise and SME infrastructure support into AI development and systems thinking. The common thread has always been the same: understanding a problem deeply enough to find a way through it.

I have been writing code since I was fifteen, although programming has never been my primary discipline — Turbo Pascal on paper, writing code and running it through truth tables before touching a machine. Languages came after that as the work required them: Perl for parsing command outputs, C# and MATLAB through formal courses, Embedded C for microcontroller work, Python when Perl turned clumsy for serial communication and three lines solved what fifty couldn't.

While I was studying Python, a message appeared on my screen inviting me to Google's Foobar challenge. I reached the final level but did not pass it. I became so absorbed by the challenge that I stopped sleeping. It was time-bounded; given enough time, I would have learned whatever I needed to solve it.

One of the projects that best illustrates how I think began with a strange client brief: build a robot arm without power.

So the end effector had to be deployed into position without motors, control systems nor sensors?

I stopped trying to solve the problem as an abstraction and tried to reproduce the desired movement physically. I used a plastic band between my fingers: two contact points orbiting each other. The fixed relationship between them already contained the mechanism I needed.

I designed a mechanism that synchronised the movement using static pulleys, concentric shafts and belts.

That is broadly how I approach problems. Sometimes the answer comes through structure: functional decomposition, research, immersion, and repeatedly reframing the brief. Others require something different — the rational mind needs to get out of the way. Painting, walking, or simply creating enough distance for an idea to arrive without being forced.

And sometimes the fastest route is creative fast failure: build it, find the wall, and learn where it actually stands.

I spent months building base66, an experiment in universal dense token compression for agent-to-agent AI communication. It did not achieve its original goal — tokenizers are already highly optimised for that particular problem — but it taught me things I could not have learned any other way.

That same curiosity is what draws me to AI.

I think about AI as a philosophical problem as much as a technical one. Neither humans nor AI have approached their highest potential. The question I keep returning to is what becomes possible at genuine symbiosis — not AI as tool, not AI as threat, but as a collaborator.

I am building toward that, one experiment at a time.

Away from screens, I paint and ride skateboards. I started painting with acrylics without any prior knowledge, learning by looking at how colour and proportion actually behave rather than how they are described.

The same principle applies to AI experiments: observe what actually happens, rather than assuming what should happen.
