When constraints become features

One thing I keep noticing across projects is that the tightest constraints tend to produce the most interesting solutions. The BackPack Arm is probably the clearest example of this from my own work.

The brief was straightforward on paper: robot arms that move, mounted on a backpack, no electricity. In practice it meant solving two problems simultaneously — fitting a mechanism inside a volume that was already fully designed, and making it work without any motors or controllers.

The spatial constraint pushed me toward a pulley-and-belt approach rather than a gear train, simply because it was thinner. That same choice then gave us something unexpected: by fixing one pulley at the shoulder and letting the belt run freely, the elbow joint orbits automatically as the arm raises. Two degrees of freedom from one input. That only happened because there was no room for a second actuator.

I’ve seen this pattern elsewhere too. On the planter project, the requirement to fit everything inside an existing case forced a component merger that actually reduced the part count. On the haptic finger, relocating the solenoids away from the fingertips — a constraint imposed by comfort — led to a cleaner transmission mechanism than the original design.

I’m not sure this is a lesson so much as a reminder: the instinct to treat a constraint as an obstacle worth removing is often worth resisting. Sometimes the constraint is doing half the design work for you.
