Design Engineer · Invireo · 2021

Watering Mechanism

Gantry mechanism for automated indoor water delivery

Brief

Design a mechanism to fit inside a planter enclosure not yet finalised — delivering water to any point across the growing area while preserving as much planting volume as possible.

My Role

Designed the gantry mechanism from scratch — carriage, rod mounts, belt tensioning, wall brackets, and cable routing — to fit within a provided enclosure. Applied TRIZ Principle #6 (Universality) across 5 successive iterations to merge parts and reduce complexity. Electronics were handled separately.

Outcome

Working prototype proving full planting area coverage. With space as the primary constraint, consolidating six parts into one was the most satisfying result — fewer components to assemble, fewer variables to track as the design landscape kept shifting.

Component Design

The mechanism starts as a collection of discrete, single-function parts — each solving one problem in isolation. The Y-axis carriage alone began as six separate components: a linear bearing block, a custom rod clamp, a 3D-printed secondary bracket, a belt clamp, a belt tensioner, and a stepper motor interface.

At this stage the shape of each part is unknown. There is no target geometry — only a set of constraints: fit within the enclosure, don't claim growing volume, stay serviceable. Form follows from working through those constraints iteratively, not from a predetermined design.

This is the state Component Design captures: the problem before consolidation. Each part doing one job. The question that follows is whether those jobs can be done by fewer parts — or none.

Full CAD assembly — mechanism integrated within the enclosure

From Six Parts to One

The central design challenge was space — every part had to justify its existence. Working instinctively toward consolidation, the design went through 5 documented iterations. TRIZ Principle #6 (Universality) is the framework that best describes what happened: making each part serve multiple functions until the part itself disappears.

The Y-Axis Carriage

5 successive iterations applying TRIZ Universality Principle — from 6 parts to 1

By the fourth iteration, three separate parts remained in the Y-axis carriage: an off-the-shelf linear bearing block, a custom rod clamp, and an interface bracket connecting the two. These were merged into a single part that simultaneously holds the linear bearings, clamps the rods, tensions the belt, and interfaces with the stepper motor — reducing the subassembly from 3 parts to 1.

Designing a single part to do this much created its own challenges: bolt access, print orientation, tolerance stackup across multiple interfaces, and ensuring the belt tensioners could be adjusted after assembly. Every function added to the part had to remain accessible without disassembling everything else.

The consolidated geometry also became reusable — a variation of the same part serves both X and Y axes. What changes is the assembly: different components attach to the same base, adapting it to each axis without redesigning the core.

Prototyping Process

  1. Rapid CAD prototyping — rough geometry and off-the-shelf components to validate the concept and mechanism layout within the enclosure constraints.
  2. Each physical iteration informed the next consolidation decision.
  3. 3D printing and physical fitment — mounting each iteration directly into the real enclosure to validate tolerances, reach, and assembly.
  4. Final assembly and handoff to Invireo for electronics integration and plant testing.

How It Works

The mechanism moves a water delivery nozzle across two axes (X and Y) to reach any point above the planting area. It is belt-driven, using GT2 pulleys and stepper motors. All motors and drive components are integrated within the planter's provided enclosure, keeping the mechanism's footprint entirely out of the planting zone.
