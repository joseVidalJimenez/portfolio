Mechanical Design · ONN / Makers Department · 2018

BackPack Arm

Fixed-Pulley Orbital Drive with Self-Contained Motion Control

Description

Designed a mechanical, human-powered robotic arm system for a video production client. Two arms mounted on a backpack, capable of full shoulder and elbow movement, with zero electrical input.

My Role

Translated the client's vision into a fixed-pulley orbital drive mechanism, designed entirely within the physical constraints of an existing backpack. Responsible for both the mechanical system and the electronics integration.

Outcome

Delivered a self-contained robotic arm system driven entirely by human power. A fixed shoulder pulley synchronises both arm and forearm movement through geometry alone — no motors, no controllers, no electrical components.

Deployed on Set

The arm was worn by a performer on set during a live video shoot. Two extended arms rise above the performer's shoulders, each holding a mounted camera, all controlled by the wearer's natural body movement.

The backpack acts as the structural base, distributing weight across the torso while keeping the mechanism self-contained. No visible cables, motors, or controls. The mechanical system is entirely internal.

This real-world deployment validated the core design goal: a fully wearable, power-free robotic arm system suitable for dynamic on-set use.

Mechanical Analysis

1. Single Input, Dual Joint Movement

The shoulder uses a coaxial arrangement: two concentric shafts sharing a single axis. One shaft seats on a bearing, forming the revolute joint the arm rotates around. The second shaft carries the driving pulley, positioned so the arm sits sandwiched between the pulley and the shoulder — transferring motion directly into the arm without additional linkages. Two degrees of freedom, one compact joint.

2. Pulley-Belt Synchronisation

A toothed timing belt connects two toothed pulleys: one fixed at the shoulder (0 DOF), one at the elbow (1 DOF). The fixed shoulder pulley acts as an orientation lock — the belt constrains the elbow pulley to maintain a constant angular relationship with it. As the arm sweeps upward, the elbow pulley orbits the fixed point while the belt enforces that lock, rotating the forearm synchronously. One cable pull coordinates both joints. No elbow actuator required.

3. Fixed Pulley, Dynamic Elbow

Most mechanisms that coordinate two joints require active control at each — gear trains, multi-link kinematic chains, or independent actuators. Here, fixing the shoulder pulley eliminates all of that. The forearm's rotation is not commanded — it is a passive consequence of the arm's position, enforced entirely by geometry. The fixed point does the work that a motor would otherwise do — no power, no signal, no moving parts beyond the belt itself.

4. Gravity-Assisted Return + Magnetic Damper

Gravity returns the arm to rest without any actuator input — the return phase requires no dedicated motor or control logic. A magnetic damper at the shoulder catches the arm in the final moment before it reaches the body, serving two distinct functions: absorbing the descent to prevent impact against the wearer, and locking the arm in place during dynamic movement so it cannot swing freely when the wearer runs or changes direction. Passive return, active hold.

Prototyping Process

Each stage had one job: prove the concept, then move on. From dozens of sketches to a settled design, through multiple cardboard mockups, through print after print refining tolerances and geometry, to a final wooden rig built to take real load. Each stage advanced only when mature enough to earn the next.

  1. Basic 2D mechanism using cardboard and pushpins. The goal was to confirm the single-input dual-joint principle before committing to any physical structure.
  2. Functional cardboard mockup to test movement timing and joint coordination in three dimensions. This revealed how the orbital forearm motion behaved under the fixed pulley constraint.
  3. CAD modelling and 3D printing for rapid geometry iteration. Each print tested specific tolerances — pulley fit, belt tension, and shaft concentricity — progressively resolving the dimensional limitations of each previous version.
  4. Wooden test rig to validate the final mechanism under realistic load. The rigidity of wood eliminated flex from the equation, isolating the mechanical behaviour of the joints from material compliance.

Electronics Integration

The camera was the arm's end-effector: the functional payload the entire mechanism was designed to carry and position. Integrating it meant treating the arm not just as a mechanical structure, but as a purpose-built housing for a live electronics system.

Camera System

An off-the-shelf wireless pan-tilt camera head serves as the arm's end-effector. Being wireless eliminated signal cable routing through the arm joints entirely, a significant simplification given the forearm's orbital motion.

Internal PCB Housing

The camera's PCB is housed internally within the 3D printed arm structure. This required the arm geometry to be designed around the electronics from the outset, not retrofitted after.

Power Routing

A single power cable runs from the arm to the backpack battery pack, managed with a Flexible Spiral Sheath that accommodates the arm's full range of motion without tangling or fatiguing at the joints.

The backpack carries the battery, the arm positions the camera, the spiral-sheathed cable bridges the two.

Key Innovation

In conventional pulley systems, both pulleys rotate — one drives, one follows. The BackPack Arm inverts this: the shoulder pulley is fixed, with zero degrees of freedom. It never rotates. That immobility is the mechanism.

As the arm raises, the elbow pulley is forced to orbit the fixed point. Because the timing belt maintains a constant angular relationship between them, and the elbow pulley is locked to the forearm, the forearm rises in perfect synchrony — not because it was commanded to, but because geometry demands it.

The input is handled by a coaxial shoulder joint: two concentric shafts sharing a single axis, one defining the arm's rotation, the other carrying the driving pulley. A single cable pull sets everything in motion. Gravity returns the arm to rest, caught at the final moment by a magnetic damper.

The result replaces independent actuators, encoders, and control logic at each joint — with a fixed pulley, a timing belt, and a magnet.
