Mechanical Design · Mauro Campisi · 2022

Universal Cable Adapter

Design Engineer — Mechatronics

The brief was simple: one cord, every country. The hard part was building an adapter that wouldn't power anything until you decided it should.

Description

One power cord. Every international socket. No loose dongles, no exposed contacts — the adapter is built into the cord itself, and the connection only goes live when you choose.

My Role

Took the project from competitive teardown through mechanism design, prototyping, and fabrication — every part 3D printed, every contact hand-placed in brass.

Outcome

A working prototype that passes through every international socket geometry and only energises on deliberate rotation — proving the mechanism concept end to end.

The Problem

The brief had a built-in contradiction. Fit every major international socket into a single adapter — and make it smaller than everything already on the market.

Existing adapters solve the multi-socket problem the obvious way: stack the geometries side by side, add sliders to select between them, and accept the bulk. The result is always a cube. The UK plug sets the floor — it's the largest footprint of any standard, and everything has to fit around it.

The more sockets you need to support, the bigger the housing gets. Shrinking it wasn't a refinement problem — it was a contradiction at the core of the brief.

Competitive Teardown

Disassembling several travel adapters revealed how the better-engineered products handled the multi-function challenge of plug heads: pivot, lock, and conduct — each action triggered at the right moment through elegantly simple parts.

The Chinese adapters used large brass plates as both guides and conductors. The Europe plug head was even more instructive — each leg held by a single screw that doubled as the contact point when the plug rotated into position. Plastic pivots handled the mechanical side. Two separate systems, each optimised for mass production.

The UCA worked from the same principle but collapsed it further. The original plug legs were extracted from the teardown sample and re-housed in a 3D printed body — the brass rods now handling pivot, location, and electrical continuity as a single element. A prototype-scale interpretation of the same logic, with one fewer system.

The Key Insight

Every adapter on the market treats the housing and the selection mechanism as separate problems. A box to fit the sockets in, and something — sliders, buttons, levers — bolted on to select between them.

The breakthrough was realising they could be the same thing.

A triangle has three faces and an inscribed circle. The three faces became the three socket types, each carrying its own plug geometry. The inscribed circle became the shaft — simultaneously the knob you turn, the rotary switch that indexes the connection, and the structural core of the housing. Rotating the knob doesn't activate a mechanism. Rotating the knob is the mechanism.

The result was a housing that couldn't be made any smaller, because every part of it was already doing two jobs.

Rotary Mechanism

The rotary selection knob is the UCA's primary mechanical contribution. Rather than repositioning plug geometry through a passive adapter, the knob indexes the cord body directly — rotating between socket positions through a detented selector integrated into the cord head. The brass contact mechanism was developed from scratch to maintain IEC‑60320 electrical continuity across all indexed positions.

Prototype plugged into a UK socket — rotary selector indexed to UK BS 1363 position.

Indexing Logic

Positive detents lock the selector at each socket position. The detent geometry was refined across multiple printed iterations — each print resolving one issue: tactile feel, end-stop alignment, or grip ergonomics. The black actuator visible in the contact photo is the printed selector slider.

Why this approach

Most international adapters treat plug geometry as fixed. The UCA treats it as variable — the plug geometry is selectable at the point of use, integrated into the cord body. The rotary switch is the mechanism that enables this: rather than carrying multiple cable types or loose adapter blocks, the user selects the base of the cord to index between connector standards. The switching action is mechanical and direct — no moving electrical components beyond the contact wipe that occurs during rotation.

Achieving this required a custom brass contact set sized and rated to IEC‑60320 — nothing off-the-shelf could provide what the rotary component needed. The result is a single cable that replaces a travel kit.

Prototyping & Validation

The UCA reached a working prototype integrating the rotary selector, hand-fabricated brass contacts, and 3D printed PLA housings. The prototype demonstrated indexed switching across socket positions and was tested for basic connection with a UK socket strip — the proof-of-function shown in the photographs below.

What was built

A complete cord assembly — rotary selector, two brass spring contacts per position, colour-coded wiring harness, and printed PLA housings in multiple iterations. Built without custom tooling; all fabrication by hand.

What was tested

Socket engagement geometry, detent indexing across all positions, contact continuity under insertion and withdrawal cycles.

What passed

Dimensional compatibility with IEC‑60320 connector geometry confirmed. Rotary indexing reliable across all socket positions tested.

Each prototype stage targeted a specific uncertainty before advancing to the next. The brass contacts and rotary mechanism were developed in parallel, combined only when both were sufficiently resolved independently.

Stage 01

Research and teardown

Baseline contact quality, switching geometry, and failure modes across current adapters. Teardowns exposed consistent failures at the contact wipe.

Stage 02

Geometry validation

Initial rotary sketches evaluated against IEC‑60320 requirements. Rotary geometry validated against pin spacing before any physical parts were made.

Stage 03

3D printed housing

Multiple iterations refined grip ergonomics, detent feel, and contact alignment. Each print resolved one dimensional issue before advancing.

Stage 04

Hand-formed assembly

Brass contacts fabricated and integrated into final housing, producing functional prototype. Completed socket engagement and indexing tests across all positions.

Outcome

This project sits at the boundary between product design and mechatronics engineering. The UCA required mechanical design, contact physics, manufacturing process selection, and iterative hardware testing — a working method José is actively deepening as he transitions into mechatronics.

Mechanical design

Designed a rotary indexing mechanism from first principles — detent geometry, end-stop alignment, grip ergonomics. Iterated across five printed housing versions. All fabrication by hand, no CNC or custom tooling.

Electrical integration

Specified and hand-fabricated brass spring contacts to IEC‑60320 geometry. Learned to reason about contact pressure, wipe length, and spring-back at prototype scale. No off-the-shelf switch component — the contact mechanism is a custom part.
